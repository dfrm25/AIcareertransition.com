How to check AI work before it leaves your hands

Most AI mistakes at work do not look like mistakes. They look like a confident paragraph with one wrong number in it, or a tidy summary that dropped the one caveat your boss cared about. You will not catch those by reading faster. You catch them with a short check you run the same way every time.

Engineers call this an eval. For everyone else, it is just your review. Here is a version that takes about five minutes and works on any tool.

Why AI mistakes are hard to spot

  • Wrong and right read the same. The writing is fluent either way. There is no shaky tone to tip you off.
  • It fills gaps. If the source does not say something, AI may guess and not tell you it guessed.
  • It drops things quietly. Summaries lose the exception, the footnote, the “except in Europe.” Nothing marks the spot where it was.
  • It agrees with you. If your prompt assumes something (“why did sales drop?”), it will usually run with the assumption, even when sales did not drop.

The five-minute review

1. Trace three claims

Pick the three that matter most: usually a number, a name, and a date. Find each one in the source. If you cannot find it, it is not true yet. This one step catches more errors than everything else combined.

2. Look for what is missing

Ask yourself what your manager would ask first. Is the answer on the page? Then ask the AI directly: “What did you leave out of the source, and why?” It is often surprisingly honest about this when asked.

3. Check the edges

Totals, date ranges, units, currencies, time zones, names spelled right, job titles current. These are boring, and they are exactly where AI slips because they look like small details.

4. Read it as the audience

Would a customer, your legal team, or the most skeptical person in the meeting object to any sentence? Read it once pretending you are them. Promises, comparisons, and anything that sounds like a guarantee deserve a second look.

5. Decide, then write one line

Pass, fix, or redo. Then write one line about what you changed. That line is the habit that turns your checking into something you can show later.

Make AI help you check

You can ask the same tool to point at its own weak spots. These three prompts do most of the work:

List every number in your answer with the exact line in the source it came from. If you calculated or inferred it, say so.
Act as a skeptical reviewer for [AUDIENCE]. Name the three sentences most likely to be wrong or challenged, and why.
Compare your summary to the source. What did you leave out that someone on [TEAM] would care about?

One honest warning: AI checking AI catches some problems, not all. It can point you to the line. You still have to look at the line.

What to hunt for, by role

Every job has its own favorite AI mistakes. Know yours.

Marketing

Claims you cannot back up (“fastest,” “number one”), customer quotes nobody said, stats with no source, last quarter’s price or offer. Check: every claim has a source link, or it gets cut.

Analytics and finance

The wrong denominator, a total counted twice, a filter that dropped a region, year-to-date mixed with monthly, percentages that do not add up. Check: recompute one number by hand.

Product

Requirements that contradict each other, skipped edge cases (empty states, errors, permissions), invented user research, acceptance criteria nobody can test. Check: each requirement traces to a note or ticket.

HR and people teams

Policy details that differ by country, outdated benefits, loaded wording in job posts or reviews. Check: compare against the current policy document, not memory.

Operations

Steps in the wrong order, missing approvals, the wrong owner, tools your company does not use. Check: walk the steps with the person who actually does the work.

Sales and customer teams

Wrong account history, a contract term that changed, a promise support cannot keep. Check: confirm anything about a specific customer in the CRM before it goes out.

The new risk: AI that edits the file directly

Copilot can now open Word, Excel, and PowerPoint inside its app for some companies, and Claude can edit Google Docs and Sheets from a sidebar. That skips the paste step, which used to be where you read everything. Two habits fill the gap:

  • End the request with “list every cell, slide, or paragraph you changed, with old and new values,” and check three.
  • In spreadsheets, click changed cells and read the formula bar. A typed number where a formula used to be is the quietest way a model breaks next month’s report. The Excel mistakes guide covers the rest.

Keep a review log

A spreadsheet with seven columns is enough. The row below is an example of the format, not a real result:

After a month, this log is the strongest proof you have. It shows you use AI, and it shows you can be trusted with it. The “what was wrong” column is the part managers remember. It also tells you what to add to your prompt so the same mistake does not come back.

Turn this into proof

Pick one task you do every week. Run it with AI, use the five-minute review, and log it. Three weeks of rows is a case study you can walk a manager through.
