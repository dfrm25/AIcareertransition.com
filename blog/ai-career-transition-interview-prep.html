AI Interview Prep for Career Changers

The interview is where vague AI claims fall apart. “I use ChatGPT every day” sounds fine on a resume and goes nowhere in a room. What lands is one specific story: the task, the tool, how you checked it, and what changed.

Direct answer

Prepare one clear story: the business problem, the AI tool, the prompt or workflow, how you checked the output, what changed, and what you learned when the model was wrong. Then prepare for the judgment questions: when you would not use AI, and how you handle confidential data.

The story structure

Situation, then workflow, then tool, then your check, then the result, then the lesson

This keeps you from sounding like you pasted AI words into your resume. It also gives the interviewer something to dig into, which is what they want. Here is a full example, written the way you would say it out loud:

“Every Monday I wrote a pipeline summary for my director. It took about an hour, mostly copying numbers from the CRM export into a doc. I set up Copilot in Excel to draft it from the export. The first week, the total looked high. I traced it and found the model had counted two internal test accounts. So I added a rule to my prompt to exclude them, and I now check three totals against the export every week before I send it. It takes about twenty minutes now. The bigger change is that my director started asking for a short ‘what moved’ section, because I had time to write it.”

Notice what is in there: a real task, a named tool, a mistake, the fix, a habit, and a result in plain numbers you can explain. Use your own numbers. If you did not time it, say “roughly” and be ready to say how you estimated.

Weak answers and strong ones

Sounds weakSounds strong
“I use ChatGPT for everything.”“I use it for first drafts of customer emails. I never send one without checking the account details in the CRM.”
“I built an AI agent.”“I set up a Copilot agent that drafts our weekly status from the tracker. I review it before it goes out.”
“AI made me 10x more productive.”“The report went from about an hour to about twenty minutes, and I added a section my manager asked for.”
“I am good at prompt engineering.”“I keep a short list of the mistakes it makes on my data and I write those into my prompts.”

Questions to rehearse, and what a good answer includes

“What do you use AI for?”

Two or three specific tasks, not a list of tools. Pick ones close to the job you are interviewing for.

“Tell me about a time AI got it wrong.”

This is the most important question, and the one people prepare least. Have a real example ready: what it got wrong, how you noticed, and what you changed so it would not happen again. Saying “it never really gets things wrong for me” is a red flag.

“When would you not use AI?”

Good answers name situations: decisions about people, anything you cannot check, confidential data in an unapproved tool, or a message where the relationship matters more than the speed.

“How do you handle sensitive data?”

Use the company-approved tool on a work account. Keep customer and employee details out of personal accounts. Know that AI chats on work accounts can be kept and reviewed.

“What would you automate here first?”

Ask them one question back about their weekly busywork, then pick something repetitive, checkable, and low-risk. Say what you would keep human-reviewed. That shows judgment, not just enthusiasm.

If they ask you to use AI live

Some interviews hand you a laptop with an AI tool and a task. They are not grading the output. They are watching how you work. Say what you are doing as you go: what you are asking for, why, and what you will check. Then actually check one thing in front of them. Catching a mistake out loud is the best thing that can happen in that exercise.

Bring proof, not hype

Bring a one-page artifact you can walk through in two minutes: the task, the prompt, a before and after, your review notes, and the result. Remove anything confidential. The portfolio examples show formats that work. If your resume has an AI bullet, make sure it matches this evidence. The resume guide shows how.

A 30-minute prep plan

  1. Ten minutes: write your main story using the structure above. Say it out loud once and time it. Aim for under two minutes.
  2. Ten minutes: write your “AI got it wrong” story and your “when I would not use AI” answer.
  3. Five minutes: pick the one artifact you will bring and remove anything confidential.
  4. Five minutes: look up which AI tools the company uses, if it is public, and think of one task in the job description you would try first.

Next step

No story yet? Start one this week with the five-minute review on a task you already do, and log it. Three weeks of that is your interview answer.
